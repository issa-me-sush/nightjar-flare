The four largest holders of FXRP are a yield vault, two lending markets, and the bridge that carries it off Flare. Not one of them puts a price on the screen. The fourth-largest holder of FXRP is the adapter taking it somewhere else.

Incentives have already been tried at scale — Flare committed billions of FLR to bringing liquidity to FXRP — and the float responded by sitting in vaults or leaving. Rented liquidity leaves when the rent stops, because the reason it was never there was not the yield. Resting real size on a public book means publishing your intention before it fills, so the people with size rationally do not show up. That is the one thing a smart contract cannot fix, because on-chain state is public by construction.

How it works

Sealed in your browser. Matched in an enclave. Settled on-chain.

  1. 01

    Deposit

    Fund a balance, decoupled in time from any order. If you funded the exact size of each trade, the transfer itself would leak it.

  2. 02

    Seal

    Your order is encrypted to the enclave’s public key before it leaves the browser. The venue stores ciphertext and one public number — how many orders are in the batch.

  3. 03

    Clear

    The enclave opens the book, finds the single price that matches the most volume, and signs the result. Everyone in the batch trades at that one price.

  4. 04

    Settle

    The contract checks the signature, re-reads the FTSO feed to bound the price itself, and requires both assets to net to zero before it moves anything.

The enclave is not something new to trust. It never holds your funds — it returns signed balance deltas, and the contract rejects them unless both assets net to exactly zero and the clearing price sits inside a band around Flare's own FTSO feed, re-read on-chain at settlement. A valid enclave signature is necessary, and deliberately not sufficient. The enclave proposes; the chain disposes.

Getting in

Pay XRP on the XRP Ledger. The payment is what funds you here.

The fourth-largest holder of FXRP is the bridge taking it off Flare. So the venue has a second door: send XRP from whatever XRPL wallet you already use, naming the Flare address to credit in the payment reference. Flare's Data Connector attests the payment, and the gateway verifies that proof on-chain before crediting anything — priced at Flare's own FTSO feed.

  1. 01

    Pay on the XRPL

    To the desk's account, with your Flare address as the payment reference. No Flare wallet needed, and no FLR.

  2. 02

    Flare attests it

    The Data Connector's validators confirm the payment settled and publish a Merkle root for the voting round.

  3. 03

    The gateway verifies the proof

    It checks the proof against that root, that the payment reached the desk's account, that the ledger transaction succeeded, and that it has not already been claimed. Only then does anything move.

  4. 04

    Seal an order

    The credited balance goes straight into a sealed batch. Arriving from the XRPL and trading without showing your hand is one path, not two products.

The design

A batch auction is the only rule that survives more than one enclave.

The obvious way to build this is a normal price-time-priority order book inside a secure enclave. It works, and it costs you the ability to ever run a second one. Price-time priority is a function of arrival order: two enclaves fed the same orders over a network see them in different sequences, produce different matches, and sign different settlements. There is nothing for them to agree on.

A uniform-price batch auction has no such dependency. It consumes a set of orders and returns the single price that matches the most volume — feed it the same set in any order and every enclave emits identical bytes. So the venue can require k independent enclaves to sign the same settlement before the chain will execute it, and the contract enforces that they are distinct and registered.

This matters more than it did two years ago. Public research in 2025 demonstrated sub-$1,000 memory-bus attacks that forge TEE attestations on both major vendors, who treat physical interposition as out of scope with no fix planned. A venue resting on exactly one machine now has a cheap, known, unpatched single point of failure. We have not solved TEE security — we chose the matching rule that lets you defend against it. Stated plainly: the quorum path is deployed and covered by six tests, and Coston2 runs one enclave today because a second needs a second host.
